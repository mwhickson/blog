Who doesn't love a good recommendation system?

Anybody ever encounter one?

Was it programmatically curated?

I'm not saying that programmatic recommendation systems don't carry some value, but it seems that each one has its sweet spot -- once you've passed that -- it seems like there's no way back.

For me, recommendation systems come into play primarily with music or other types of internet media.

YouTube and Spotify are the two that I fight with most regularly. Netflix? Once upon a time. I don't watch much there anymore.

Fight with?

But why you ask?

Well, I don't think that I'm all that special when it comes to my preferences regarding music (and other more general media); however, my tastes might be perceived as covering a lot of ground.

I understand that presents a challenge for the recommendation systems of yore. The surprise is that it seems like modern recommendation systems really spiral into uselessness.

It seems quite odd that I feel like the old, simple preference-based systems tried to do less and ended up accomplishing more.

Generally, my criteria for things I like to see suggested comes down to:

- content by creators I enjoy
- content similar to content created by my favourite creators
- fresh content
- older content that I have not yet encountered
- content produced in languages I understand

Doesn't seem like so much to ask for. Does it?

Bonus points if the recommendation engines are capable of:

- not displaying the same content more than once
- keeping content length similar to content I watch (I like content between 5-30 minutes in length, with some exceptions, which I should be easy to determine, since those exceptions are all within a specific genre/content format)
- occasional reminders of content I have already heard/seen, but not in some time (weeks or months at least -- most certainly not just days, hours or mere minutes)
- a way to remove content I tried, but did not like, from consideration

What I absolutely do not want is:

- the latest "popular" content (I am not a sports guy. I am not a car guy. I don't like "funny" animal or "fail" videos. I don't like trailers. I don't want your rabid political ramblings -- regardless of your persuasion. There's more, but let's start there at a bare minimum.)
- a full page of recommendations based on a singular piece of content -- especially a piece I just viewed
- recommendations for content in languages I have never consumed content of any sort in
- AI generated content (though content about AI is fine)

I accept that demographically popular content is low-hanging fruit for any recommendation engine.

I willinging attempt to curtail the onslaught of that popular, though not popular with me, content.

- I log in with an account on the service in question (even when it's not required).
- I subscribe to content creators.
- I like (and less often dislike) content to actively tell you what I want more of.
- I hide/reject recommendations (which I believe to be different from disliking content) for content/creators that I don't want to see to tell you what I want less of.

This seems largely effective at the moment, but has not always been so in the past, and I suspect I'll reach the tipping point I alluded to earlier where my recommendations go completely off the rails.

Now that unfortunately, but fairly, still leaves recommendations that are probably well-meaning, but woefully off the mark a significant portion of the time.

The most obvious misuse (abuse?) of user preference data is letting subscriptions, likes, or even just listens/views queue up everything that that a particular content creator has ever produced.

Really? Why would you do that?

I just subscribed and/or liked the content of that creator. Fair enough. That indicates I could be interested in the other material they have produced.

On the other hand, unless your UI is garbage, I can easily find their content library. That library is probably even organized to some degree, making that cluster of recommendations a particularly large pet peeve of mine because it is simply filler. The illusion of recommendations intended to engage and support discovery of the next amazing thing.

It is especially frustrating when a number of the recommendations are also content that I just listened to or watched -- sometimes even appearing more than once! I'll admit that I can see the value of re-recommending things you know I already like to permit re-discovery -- if significant time had passed and you don't offer up favourites/liked/history listing features on your service. But those are core features of any media site, aren't they?

Next up on the frustration train is the "kid in a candy store" approach where similar content overwhelms the recommendation feed.

I get it, it's probably the next simplest thing to do, and the service can use my feedback to generate more recommendation detail for me -- and other users -- depending on how those recommendations pan out.

However, just because I eat a candy bar, it does not mean I want to eat one hundred candy bars -- even if they are from different manufacturers and/or contain different tasty goodies.

Don't get me wrong! I don't mind if I start to see thematically similar content based on my listen/watch history. I prefer it!

Having said that, it doesn't take long before anybody with non-myopic interests should provide more than ample data for a series of themes to emerge in recommendations and being swamped in a single interest is no longer the only option.

Ultimately, a lot of these frustrations could be resolved by just allowing me to opt out of some of them, or even just explicitly tell you what I want more of and less of.

I'm going to take a moment to pick on YouTube, because it's particularly bad in this area, even though it provides the illusion of a number of options to help.

On YouTube, I can:

- request not to see a specific video recommendation -- even supplying a (lacklustre) reason for why I don't want the recommendation
- request not to see recommendations from a particular channel
- dislike a video

Despite being able to like/subscribe and having the appearence of removing recommendations there are several issues that still arise:

- "Show fewer Shorts" -- How about "Show NO Shorts"? I'm sure there's a revenue stream somewhere that explains that option, but that has nothing to do with my preferences.
- It's unclear what removing a recommendation for a content creator you follow/like does... Further complicated if they're a musician who shows up in a "Mix" or a "Short"...
- Despite removing/blocking/sternly disapproving of recommendations... they're still likely to show up again. Maybe not today... Maybe not tomorrow... But sooner than you'd like (which would be never)

The sad thing is, the answer is dead simple, and it predates recommendation engines...

It's user preferences.

Specific user preferences alongside recommendation engines could further (and probably dramatically) improve the user experience.

Things like the following would allow explicit opt-in to refine recommendations, and help identify specific computational costs to perform these filters:

- Content Rating (G, PG, AA, R, etc.)
- Content Length (minimum/maximum guidelines)
- Tag-based content/genre preferences for show/hide (This is a classic approach! YouTube, Spotify and others even feed back your most listened to/watched genres!)
- Enable/disable specialty content types (e.g. Shorts, Mixes, Playlists, etc.)
- Content priority (age, topic, by followed, etc.)

Now, I'm not naive enough to believe that user satisfaction is the priority of any streaming service.

I acknowledge that monetization through advertising, or better still, service subscription is almost certainly a more central focus of these services.

Users, and paying users at that, are the Holy Grail of subscription services.

I'd just like to think that you attract users, and more ardent users at that, by not aggravating them into subscribing.

For instance, I don't watch Netflix much anymore at all. It's still in the household because other family members do. I simply don't find it worth my time to scroll through row after row of content that Netflix wants me to watch, rather than content I would like to watch. To see the same show or movie listed again and again under wildly different categories (and increasingly specific, yet charmingly meaningless categories), all while fighting off a lack of any real content description and jarring autoplaying previews... It just got to be more irritation than enjoyment. It certainly became obvious that my limited preferences and watch history were most definitely not queueing up content I wanted to see!

The same holds true for Spotify. Listening to podcasts with their own sponsors, interrupted by Spotify advertising, doesn't make fighting against the afterthought UI only a music streaming service could shoehorn in worth the effort. That doesn't begin to cover the jarring advertisements for Spotify or promoted artists being injected into my listening stream. The blatant disregard for my preferences when a country music themed ad is injected into my metal stream, or a rap themed ad is injected into my classical stream quickly illustrate that my interests are not a consideration when it comes time for a buck to be made.

If these services don't care enough to make themselves the best of the best when they're striving to get me to lay down my money, what is supposed to make me think that they're going to get better once I have subscribed?

In fact, other than trimming ads, they typially don't. Not these days.

I have subscribed to just about every mainstream streaming service at least once, but the perks of doing so just don't outweigh the benefits anymore.

With a subscription, I get ad-free content consumption, but still have to fight to find content I want to consume -- and to keep a healthy, interesting stream of that content in front of me.

All that because the recommendation engine has usurped something as basic as user preference settings.

These services would rather guess wrong, and less charitably, force their own sponsored content down my throat, than simply let me clearly and unambiguously tell them what I want.

I had, and sometimes still have, a better time these days with run-of-the-mill broadcast television and local radio.

Let that sink in...

Services that cater to a broader public, that operate in a one-way format, that have largely been put out to pasture, offer a more pleasant and reliable means of consuming pre-existing and upcoming new content.

And, with that, I'll end this rant before it rambles any further.

I'd just like you, dear reader, to consider what you want from your streaming services.

I'd leave a note for any streaming service representatives here, but they're either already aware and unable/unwilling to do anything about this, or just not going to stumble across this.
